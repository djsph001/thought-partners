Something strange is happening at the edge of the AI boom.

In towns across the country, people who agree on almost nothing are finding something they agree on. Left and right. Rural and suburban. People who would never stand in the same room otherwise are standing together to oppose the same thing: a data center.

The reporting on this is striking precisely because the alliance is so unlikely. The Associated Press recently described the left and the right uniting against AI data centers — the same two camps that cannot agree on a weather forecast finding common cause over a building.

But look closer at what they are actually opposing.

Whatever they may think about the models or the chatbots, the immediate object of the fight is physical.

They are opposing a very large building that will draw electricity, use water, consume land, and change the character of a place.

That distinction is the whole story.

---

## The Backlash Is Not About the Technology

Calling this an "AI backlash" gets the object wrong.

The people fighting a data-center proposal are not, for the most part, arguing about intelligence. They are arguing about a power bill, a well, a property line, a noise level, a road, a school district's tax base.

These are **resource disputes** wearing a technology's clothes.

That reframing matters, because it changes what would settle the argument. If the dispute were really about AI — about whether the technology is good or dangerous — then no local hearing could ever resolve it, and no local hearing should. But if the dispute is about water and electricity and who pays, then it is, in principle, resolvable. It is a negotiation over measurable things.

The mistake is treating a resource dispute as a philosophical one — or the reverse.

---

## Two Sides, Two Kinds of Claims

When a data center is proposed, the two sides do not merely disagree. They are making different kinds of claims, and they are rarely held to the same standard.

One side says: jobs, tax revenue, investment, a future that includes the town rather than passing it by.

The other side says: electricity prices, water stress, land use, noise, grid costs that fall on everyone while the benefits accrue to someone else.

Both of those statements contain claims that can be checked. How many jobs, and for whom, and for how long? How much water, and during which months? Who pays for the transmission line? What happens to rates when the facility draws at peak?

And both sides contain claims that are harder to check — predictions, promises, fears.

The right move here is not to pick a side. It is to separate the two kinds of claims, because a dispute cannot be settled while a verifiable claim is being treated as a slogan and an unverifiable fear is being treated as a fact.

---

## The Split That Breaks the Story

The cleanest proof that this is not "workers versus technology" is that workers are not on one side.

A data center is a construction project before it is a computing facility. That means it employs people — the same trades that build anything large. For those workers, the data center is work, a paycheck, a union job.

For other workers — the ones whose own tasks the technology is ultimately meant to automate — the same facility looks like a threat. Not to their town's character, but to their specific place in the economy.

So the familiar frame falls apart. It is not the workers against the machines. It is some workers and other workers, in the same economy, reading the same building in opposite ways.

That is not an inconvenience for the story. It is the story. Benefits and costs are not falling on the same people, and pretending otherwise is what turns an honest dispute into a cartoon.

---

## Who Gets to Decide?

There is one more asymmetry underneath the others, and it is the one that matters most.

The benefits of a data-center buildout can extend nationally or flow to companies and investors — more computing capacity, economic growth, an industry's future secured. Some benefits are local too: construction work, tax revenue, infrastructure investment. But many of the costs are intensely local: the water, the grid, the land, the noise, the rate hikes.

Those are not the same scale, and they are not the same people.

That is the question hiding inside every one of these fights, stated plainly:

**Who gets to decide whether the bargain is fair, when the benefits may be national or corporate and many of the costs are local?**

It is not enough to establish the facts. A community can know exactly how much water a facility will draw and still be left with the harder question — not what is true, but who decides.

A fair process is not the one where the louder side wins. It is the one where the people who bear the cost have a real seat at the decision, and where both sides' claims are checked against evidence rather than asserted at volume.

---

## The Question That Does the Work

The work, in every one of these disputes, comes down to four questions, asked in order:

**What is actually true? Who bears the cost? Who receives the benefit? What would a fair decision process look like?**

That is **the ledger**, and the last question is the one most often skipped.

The first three are questions of fact — measurable, checkable, worth arguing over honestly. The fourth is a question of governance. It asks who has standing, whose voice counts, and whether the people living with the cost get a genuine say in the trade.

Skip the fourth question and the first three will not save you. You can know the water numbers down to the gallon and still make the wrong decision, over the objections of the people who will live with it.

The argument over data centers is going to get louder before it gets quieter. There are more facilities coming, not fewer, and the pattern is already clear: a proposal, a hearing, two sides talking past each other, and a technology debate standing in for what is really a negotiation over water, power, cost — and power in a different sense: the power to decide.

The people who get this right will not be the ones with the loudest view of AI. They will be the ones who can separate a claim from the evidence for it, who can say who is bearing which cost — and who insist that the people carrying the local burden get a real seat at the table where the national bargain is made.
