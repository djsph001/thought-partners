When a company announces workforce reductions while discussing artificial intelligence, what has it actually told the public?

It may be investing in AI. It may expect automation to improve productivity. It may be reducing staffing requirements. Or it may be replacing work previously performed by employees whose positions are being eliminated.

These are different claims. They should not be treated as interchangeable.

An employer's statement about artificial intelligence is evidence of what the employer reported. It is not, by itself, evidence that artificial intelligence caused a worker's displacement.

The Emergence Institute is beginning a limited examination of corporate disclosures to test how clearly companies distinguish these claims.

**This article publishes our method and predictions, not our findings.**

## From accountability to measurement

Our October essay, [*The Verification Gap*](/blog/the-verification-gap), examined a governance problem: employers may possess much of the evidence needed to assess their own explanations for workforce reductions. Our earlier investigation, [*When Does a Layoff Become an AI Layoff?*](/blog/when-does-a-layoff-become-an-ai-layoff), traced that difficulty in a single case.

This investigation takes a narrower approach.

Before asking whether AI actually caused particular layoffs, we want to establish what companies explicitly disclosed in a particular category of regulatory filing.

The distinction is essential. A company can describe AI as a strategic investment without saying it eliminated jobs. It can describe efficiency improvements without identifying the employees whose work was replaced.

If we collapse those statements into a single category called *AI layoffs*, we risk confusing a corporate explanation with a demonstrated cause.

## The sample and its limitations

Our starting point is a secondary tally published by FourWeekMBA identifying twelve companies said to have mentioned AI in connection with restructuring disclosures under Item 2.05 of Form 8-K during 2026.

This is **not a random sample of corporate layoffs**. It is a selected list derived from another publication.

Consequently, our investigation cannot estimate how frequently employers generally attribute layoffs to AI, nor can it establish the proportion of layoffs actually caused by automation.

We will check the selected filings against the underlying SEC documents rather than accept the secondary tally as evidence of what each filing says.

One company, Cisco, was examined before the predictions below were recorded and is designated as a pilot. It is excluded from the eleven-filing scored cohort.

The remaining eleven Item 2.05 sections have not yet been coded.

## Three questions, three codes

Each filing will be examined for three distinct statements:

**G — AI is an investment priority.** Does the company explicitly identify artificial intelligence as an investment or strategic priority?

**H — AI reduced staffing needs.** Does the company explicitly attribute reduced staffing requirements to AI?

**I — AI performs work previously done by affected employees.** Does the company explicitly say AI is performing work formerly performed by the employees affected by the reduction?

Each question is coded separately as:

- **Explicit:** The statement appears expressly in the relevant section.
- **Absent from section:** The section does not make the statement.
- **Unclear:** The wording does not permit a confident classification.

A broad claim about productivity, for example, does not automatically establish reduced staffing needs or replacement of particular employees' work.

The coders must record the supporting sentence or paragraph for their classifications.

Our source boundary is deliberately strict: **only Item 2.05 text counts**. Press releases, exhibits, and other sections of the same filing are excluded from the primary coding exercise.

We will also check whether Item 2.05 contains the terms *AI*, *artificial intelligence*, or *agentic*. This checks the secondary tally's selection criterion; it is not itself evidence of displacement.

## What the pilot looked like

Cisco's Item 2.05, in its May 13, 2026 Form 8-K, says the restructuring is meant to "invest in key growth opportunities including silicon, optics, security and artificial intelligence (AI)." It describes the charges as severance, termination benefits, and other costs.

Under our rules, that is **Explicit** for G, and **Absent from section** for both H and I.

This is one filing, coded by one coder. It shows how the rules operate. It is not a finding about employers.

## Our predictions, recorded before scoring

On November 23, 2026, the human research lead recorded the following expectations for the eleven scored filings in the research workbook:

| Claim | Predicted explicit disclosures |
|---|---:|
| G — AI is an investment priority | **5 of 11** |
| H — AI reduced staffing needs | **2 of 11** |
| I — AI performs affected employees' former work | **0 of 11** |

These numbers are forecasts, not observations.

They represent our expectations about what the selected Item 2.05 sections will explicitly state, not estimates of what happened inside the companies.

We will compare each prediction with the coded count for that claim and report the difference. There is no pass-or-fail threshold, and we will not revise a prediction after scoring begins.

A filing classified as *Absent from section* does not prove the underlying event did not occur. It establishes only that the particular claim was not made within our defined source boundary.

Likewise, an explicit statement does not independently verify the company's causal explanation.

## What was known before the predictions

Transparency requires us to describe the investigation's development.

Protocol version 1 was written on November 22, 2026, and revised that day. The revision replaced an earlier single yes-or-no question about AI performing eliminated roles with three separate claims.

Cisco's Item 2.05 section was examined before the predictions were recorded. Cisco is therefore a pilot, not part of the prospective scored cohort.

The research team had also encountered the published secondary tally before the predictions were formed. The predictions are consequently informed by prior exposure to the selected sample's description.

This is not a fully blinded study or an independent test of a randomly selected population.

The protocol was developed privately on November 22. The predictions were supplied on November 23. The public publication date, once the protocol is actually released, will establish when these commitments became publicly inspectable.

We will not present the earlier private development date as though it were the public registration date.

## Independent coding and corrections

A second coder will examine the same primary filing text without seeing the first coder's classifications.

Both original sets of classifications will be preserved. Disagreements and their resolutions will be recorded rather than silently overwritten.

The protocol also requires attention to document identity. An amended 8-K may contain relevant Item 2.05 material absent from an earlier filing. Cloudflare, for example, has a specified amended filing in the research protocol.

Where a selected filing cannot be located or its Item 2.05 boundary cannot be confirmed, the problem will be documented. A missing or inconsistent source will not be converted into an affirmative finding.

Five accession numbers still require confirmation. The independent second-coder pass has not begun.

After scoring begins, any coding correction must carry a dated explanation. Changes to the protocol itself must be distinguished from corrections made under the existing rules.

## What this investigation can establish

The completed study should allow us to report, within this selected cohort, how often the three statements appear explicitly in the defined sections.

It may also identify cases where the secondary tally's characterization differs from the underlying filing.

It cannot, on its own, establish that AI caused a particular workforce reduction.

That would require a different evidentiary investigation, potentially involving operational records, staffing decisions, deployment timelines, and alternative explanations.

A later stage may compare each filing with press coverage of the same workforce reduction. That comparison is outside the present protocol.

The distinction between these stages is part of the research design, not a limitation to conceal.

## Why the distinction matters

Lawmakers are beginning to ask for more information about technology-related workforce reductions. Connecticut now requires employers filing federal WARN notices whether layoffs are related to AI or another technological change. California's SB 951, signed September 30, 2026, will require additional detail when a reduction is substantially caused by AI or automation, beginning January 1, 2027.

Yet additional disclosure does not automatically produce reliable causal evidence. In both approaches, the employer makes the first assessment.

A reporting system must distinguish what a company asserts, what its records support, and what an independent reviewer can verify.

Our investigation begins with the first of those questions.

We are publishing the questions, boundaries, classifications, and predictions before scoring the remaining eleven filings so that readers can later compare the reported findings against the method we committed to use.

**The first obligation of an accountability system is not to produce a compelling explanation. It is to preserve the difference between a claim and the evidence needed to test it.**
