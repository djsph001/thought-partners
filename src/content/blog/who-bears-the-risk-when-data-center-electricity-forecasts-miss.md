## The Cost of Anticipating Demand

Electricity infrastructure is built for a future that cannot be known with certainty.

Utilities must anticipate how much electricity customers will need, where that demand will occur, and which investments will be necessary to serve it. These decisions can involve substantial financial commitments made years before the anticipated demand arrives.

The expansion of artificial intelligence has made this planning problem more consequential. Data centers require electricity at a scale that can influence regional infrastructure decisions, wholesale markets, and the costs ultimately borne by customers.

But the central question is not simply how much electricity AI will consume.

It is who bears the financial consequences when actual demand differs from the expectations used to justify investment.

There are two directions in which that uncertainty matters.

If a utility prepares infrastructure for demand that does not materialize, someone must bear the costs of investments made in anticipation of that demand.

If substantial electricity demand arrives, the resulting pressure on generation and wholesale markets may create costs extending beyond the customers responsible for the new load.

These are different problems. They arise through different mechanisms, and they are not necessarily addressed by the same regulatory instruments.

## When the Forecast Changes

The Electric Power Research Institute's 2026 projections illustrate the uncertainty confronting electricity planners.

EPRI projects that data centers could account for 9% to 17% of U.S. electricity consumption by 2030, up from 4% to 5% today. EPRI describes these projections as about 60% higher than its 2024 projections.

That is a substantial change in expectations over a relatively short period.

It is not, however, proof that an earlier forecast failed.

The year 2030 has not arrived. The revision reflects a change in projected demand, not a demonstrated difference between projected and realized consumption.

This distinction matters because forecasts influence decisions before their accuracy can be fully evaluated. Utilities and regulators must assess proposed investments while important assumptions remain uncertain.

A higher projection may support additional planning. It does not, by itself, establish that every proposed investment is necessary or that the projected demand will materialize at the expected location and time.

Likewise, a lower projection would not automatically prove that previously approved infrastructure was unjustified.

The governance problem is therefore not how to eliminate uncertainty. It is how to make consequential decisions under uncertainty while assigning responsibility for the risks those decisions create.

## When Demand Arrives, Costs Can Spread

One concern is what happens when substantial new electricity demand actually enters the system.

A preliminary, non-peer-reviewed working paper from the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas examines the effects of data-center demand on wholesale electricity markets using an hourly least-cost dispatch model.

In its revised October 2026 version, the researchers estimate that data-center demand increased national wholesale electricity prices by 6.9% by 2025, with a modeled effect of 15.0% in Northern Virginia. Under the paper's preferred scenario, the model projects a 12.3% national increase by 2028.

These are counterfactual model estimates and projections, not direct measurements of observed price increases caused by data centers. The figures are sensitive to assumptions and scenarios, and earlier versions of the research reported different headline estimates.

Under the preferred scenario, the model also estimates that data-center demand increases payments to electricity generators by approximately $186.7 billion over 2021–2028, a period that includes projected years. Of that amount, roughly $94.5 billion—about half—represents inframarginal rents: additional payments on electricity generation that would have occurred without the added data-center demand. The remaining increase reflects the modeled cost of supplying additional electricity.

The model treats future electricity supply as exogenous over its relatively short forecast horizon. The researchers note that lengthy interconnection queues mean new supply added between 2026 and 2028 is likely not a response to the recent AI demand surge.

They also caution that long-term contracts, financial hedges, and cost-of-service recovery can attenuate or delay the transmission of wholesale-market effects into retail electricity rates.

These qualifications matter. A modeled wholesale-price increase should not be presented as an equivalent increase in household electricity bills.

There is, however, an observed market development worth examining.

In the PJM regional electricity market, capacity auction clearing prices rose from $28.92 per megawatt-day for the 2024/25 delivery year to $269.92 for 2025/26, according to PJM's published Base Residual Auction results. The 2025/26 price applied to most of the PJM footprint; certain constrained zones cleared at higher prices.

These are prices for capacity commitments, not household electricity rates.

PJM also identified other factors affecting the auction, including a 3,243 MW increase in forecasted load, an increase in the installed reserve margin from 14.7% to 17.8%, and a significant decrease in supply from generation retirements.

Monitoring Analytics, PJM's independent market monitor, identifies data-center load growth as the primary reason for recent and expected capacity-market conditions, including high prices.

The auction prices are observed outcomes. The attribution of those conditions to data-center growth is the market monitor's assessment. Neither establishes that data centers alone caused the entire price increase.

Retail-price research offers another perspective.

A non-peer-reviewed working paper from MIT's Center for Energy and Environmental Policy Research estimates that data-center entry between 2010 and 2024 increased average retail electricity prices by 2.7% relative to utilities without entry. Its estimated effects include 2.1% for residential customers and 5.6% among investor-owned utilities.

A separate preprint by authors affiliated with EPRI and Watershed examines 2015–2024 and, using a different causal-identification approach, estimates that data centers modestly reduced average retail electricity rates. Its peer-review status has not been established here.

These are selected studies, not a comprehensive survey of the literature.

EPRI also produced the demand projections discussed earlier. Two authors of EPRI's 2026 forecast also contributed to the retail-rate preprint: Geoffrey Blanford, affiliated with EPRI, and John Bistline, whose affiliation on the preprint is Watershed. EPRI describes itself as an independent nonprofit whose research is funded by members.

Other sources also have institutional contexts worth identifying. The Dallas Fed working paper states that its authors' views do not necessarily reflect those of the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas or the Federal Reserve System. An author of the MIT paper acknowledges Institute for Humane Studies funding for data purchase. Monitoring Analytics describes itself as PJM's independent market monitor.

These relationships do not determine whether the findings are correct. They are relevant context for assessing the evidence.

The MIT and EPRI findings should not be treated as a simple contest in which one study must be correct and the other mistaken. Their periods and identification strategies differ.

Together, these studies illustrate why the economic consequences of data-center electricity demand cannot be reduced to a single percentage.

Wholesale prices, capacity auction outcomes, and retail electricity rates measure different things. Their relationships matter, but they are not interchangeable.

## When Expected Demand Does Not Arrive

There is another risk, moving in the opposite direction.

Suppose a utility prepares to serve a large data center. It may need to expand facilities, secure capacity, or make other commitments based on the customer's projected electricity requirements.

What happens if the customer ultimately uses substantially less electricity than anticipated, delays operations, or abandons the project?

The investments may already have been made.

Without adequate protections, some costs could become difficult to recover from the customer whose anticipated demand helped justify them.

This is where the regulatory treatment of large electricity customers becomes especially important.

One documented example is AEP Ohio's data-center tariff, adopted through proceedings before the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio.

The commission's stated purpose was to protect existing customers from bearing costs associated with adding new large electricity loads.

The adopted framework includes several protections intended to assign specified obligations to qualifying large customers.

Among them is an 85% minimum-demand mechanism, under which covered customers face payment obligations tied to a substantial portion of their contracted demand. The arrangement also includes a contractual period extending through the ramp-up phase and eight additional years, together with exit-related protections and collateral requirements.

These provisions place financial obligations on the requesting customer. Reading them as protection against under-consumption is this essay's interpretation, not the commission's stated purpose.

The tariff does not eliminate forecasting uncertainty.

Instead, it attempts to allocate particular financial obligations before the uncertainty is resolved.

The commission's stated objective was customer protection and cost allocation, not the creation of a general system for correcting inaccurate electricity forecasts. Interpreting the arrangement as one response to forecast-related financial exposure is this essay's analytical framing.

Nor does the adopted tariff demonstrate that its protections have already worked in practice. The evidence establishes the design of the obligations, while actual enforcement outcomes—including defaults, collateral draws, or exit-fee collections—remain unverified.

This essay examines one adopted tariff. Other jurisdictions are not assessed here.

The AEP Ohio case therefore illustrates a specific regulatory response, not an established national pattern.

## The Boundary of the Protection

The AEP Ohio example also reveals an important limitation.

An 85% minimum-demand obligation is principally relevant when a covered customer consumes less electricity than the contracted level.

It does not directly resolve the different problem of rapidly increasing demand affecting wholesale electricity markets.

These risks point in different directions.

One concerns the possibility that infrastructure commitments exceed the demand eventually realized.

The other concerns the effects of substantial demand entering electricity markets and influencing prices, capacity requirements, and payments to generators.

A retail tariff designed to protect existing customers from particular costs associated with a new large load cannot, by itself, establish that broader wholesale-market effects have been addressed.

There are also jurisdictional boundaries.

State regulators exercise authority over important aspects of retail electricity service, while federal regulation governs substantial parts of wholesale electricity markets and interstate transmission.

An October 2026 Federal Energy Regulatory Commission order concerning PJM's proposed large-load curtailment arrangements illustrates the importance of this division. FERC rejected most of the proposed framework in part because it appeared to impose obligations directly on new large loads that were predominantly retail customers. The commission retained more limited provisions concerning a 50-megawatt definition and a registry.

The decision does not mean that federal and state regulators lack tools to address data-center demand.

It illustrates that the authority to impose a particular obligation depends on the regulatory jurisdiction involved.

Consequently, a measure adopted at one level of the electricity system should not be presented as a comprehensive solution to risks arising elsewhere.

## What Would Accountability Require?

The debate over data-center electricity demand often begins with competing forecasts.

How much electricity will AI require? How quickly will demand grow? How many new facilities will actually be built?

These are important questions, but they do not exhaust the governance problem.

Even an accurate aggregate forecast may conceal substantial differences between locations, customers, and the timing of individual projects.

And even when regulators agree that demand is likely to increase, they must still decide which financial commitments are justified and how their consequences should be distributed.

The AEP Ohio case suggests several questions worth asking of any proposed arrangement.

First, what specific risk is the instrument designed to address? A minimum-payment obligation aimed at protecting against under-consumption should not be evaluated as though it were intended to prevent wholesale-price increases.

Second, who is legally responsible for the relevant costs? The existence of a contractual obligation is meaningful, but its scope matters. It may apply only to particular customers, facilities, or categories of load.

Third, how can the public determine whether the protections operate as intended? An adopted tariff establishes rules. Evidence about compliance, enforcement, and actual cost recovery is needed to evaluate their performance.

Finally, which risks remain outside the instrument's reach?

These questions are not arguments for a single national solution. They are a way to distinguish a documented protection from a broader claim that the electricity system has been insulated against uncertain demand.

The same discipline should apply to economic research. Forecasts should be identified as forecasts, model estimates as estimates, and observed outcomes as observations. Different studies should not be combined into a single conclusion when their methods and measured outcomes differ.

## Who Ultimately Bears the Risk?

The expansion of AI infrastructure is forcing electricity planners to make consequential decisions under conditions of uncertainty.

EPRI's demand projections have changed substantially. Economic studies identify different potential effects across wholesale and retail markets. In Ohio, one state regulator has adopted a tariff assigning specified obligations to qualifying large customers.

But these developments do not describe one unified risk.

When anticipated demand does not arrive, the concern may be who pays for commitments made to serve it.

When substantial demand does arrive, the concern may be how its effects move through electricity markets and which participants bear or benefit from the resulting changes.

AEP Ohio's adopted tariff offers one concrete example of how regulators can assign certain obligations to large customers. It does not demonstrate that every cost has been transferred, that enforcement has succeeded, or that wholesale-market exposure has disappeared.

The broader lesson is that accountability requires more than increasingly sophisticated forecasts.

It requires identifying the decisions made in reliance on those forecasts, the financial obligations created by those decisions, and the evidence needed to determine whether the resulting protections work.

The question is not only how much electricity data centers will need. It is who makes commitments on the strength of that expectation, who bears the consequences when reality differs, and who is responsible for verifying the result.
