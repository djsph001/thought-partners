Here is a sentence worth sitting with: before it will issue another permit, Texas wants a data center to cover its own grid costs, lower its neighbors' electricity bills, and leave the community's water alone.

Not answer questions about those things. Be subject to conditions around them.

That is Texas again, in September — the fourth move in a sequence that has run since June, from protecting ratepayers to auditing the grid queue to policing water, and now to halting permits. On September 21, Governor Greg Abbott directed the state's environmental commission to stop issuing permits to data centers until the state's grid and water audits are finished. This latest directive broadened the consequence from the interconnection queue to the wider permitting process, gathering several substantive expectations into the same gate.

---

## From Questions to Conditions

The questions I wrote about earlier were the right ones: is the project paying its own way? Is it taking water the community relies on? Who owns it? Texas has now added something those questions did not contain — a list of conditions.

Cover your own electrical infrastructure, so the cost does not land on residential bills. Show that your development lowers those bills. Complete the grid audit. Do not take water the community needs. Report your electricity and water use, with consequences for not reporting. Respect the setbacks that protect the people who live nearby.

The shift is small on the page and everything in practice. Questions can be answered and set aside. Conditions are either met or they are not. Most of the policy ingredients were already visible in June — cover your own infrastructure, protect the water, mind the neighbors. What changed over the summer was the enforcement architecture: connecting those ingredients to audits, interconnection, water compliance, and finally permitting. The direction of the arrow has turned, from *tell us what you are doing* to *meet these standards before you move forward*.

---

## What the Commons Charges

There is an old idea underneath this, and I want to name it once and then get out of its way.

Grid capacity and water availability are different systems, but both can become locally scarce inputs whose costs are shared beyond the individual project. A company that builds on them is not simply buying a service. It is taking access to something the public holds in common. The question a society eventually has to answer is what it charges for that access — what a builder must give back before it is allowed to take. The six conditions are Texas's first draft of an answer.

The rest of this is not about that idea. It is about whether the draft has teeth.

---

## Information, Condition, Enforcement

I have argued before that a verification gate can close the information gap without touching anything else. A project can be forced to answer questions and still be allowed to build, because answering and being stopped are not the same act. The new move matters because it reaches for a different step.

The sequence that decides whether any of this is real has three parts, and they are not the same thing: **information** — what a project must disclose; **condition** — what a project must satisfy; **enforcement** — what actually happens to a project that does not.

Texas has the first two. The disclosures were already running from August. The conditions are new. What it does not yet have is the third.

---

## The Part That Is Not Yet Codified

Here is where the record refuses to be simple.

The picture is more complicated than law versus not-law. Some pieces already rest on statute: the water-use reporting that data centers have to file is a requirement of the Texas Water Code, and Abbott's September order directed the water board to enforce penalties for failing to comply with existing law, not to invent a new one. Other pieces are a governor telling agencies how to use authority they already hold — the audit, the cost allocation. Still others are protections Abbott says he wants the legislature to codify or expand next session.

That mixed record is the honest version, and it is the one that matters. A requirement written into the water code is harder to unwind than a directive. A directive is harder to unwind than a promise to seek legislation. Texas has built its gate out of all three materials at once — which is exactly why it is still too early to say how much of it will hold.

---

## The Gate Names Itself, Not the Rule

There is a second gap, and it is the one that will decide whether any of this outlasts the news cycle.

The halt says: no permits until the audits are done. It does not say what the audits must find, or how a project demonstrates it has met a condition, or what evidence is sufficient to unlock construction. There is a compliance-reporting deadline in October, but it is a check-in, not an expiration. The grid operator has said the audit could run into December.

A developer reading the directive today knows what is being asked. It does not know what would satisfy it. That is not a detail; it is the difference between a rule and a pause. A rule can be met. A pause can only be waited out.

---

## The Question That Will Now Be Answered for Us

The significance of the Texas experiment is straightforward: it tests whether access to constrained public infrastructure can be conditioned on obligations rather than disclosure alone. Whether that produces durable cost-shifting and resource protection is now an empirical question.

What remains to be seen is whether the conditions are the gate itself, or just its announcement. Whether *lower the bills* and *leave the water* become things a project must demonstrate before it builds, or things a governor said while the permits were paused. Whether the commons, having named its price, is actually able to collect it.

That is the difference between a state that has begun to charge for access and a state that has begun to say it will. The next few months — the October filings, the December report, the legislative session after that — will tell us which one Texas is turning out to be.

**The price of access is being named. Whether it is being collected is the open question.**
